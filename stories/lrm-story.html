What Are Large Reasoning Models?

Models that think before they talk, how they are trained, and what the thinking costs

Babak RezaeeDaryakenari

Leiden University

An LLM predicts the next token, then the next

Give it a prompt and it predicts whatever word statistically fits next, then outputs a token, and another, and another: pattern matching that cranks out human-like text.

An LRM thinks before it talks

It still outputs tokens, but first it sketches a plan, weighs options, and double-checks calculations in a sandbox. It plans, evaluates what comes back, and only then answers.

Reflex is fine for a post, not for shell companies

For "write a fun social media post," the LLM's reflex is usually fine. For "debug this gnarly stack trace" or "trace my cash flow through four different shell companies," reflex is not enough.

Thinking tests hypotheses and discards dead ends

The internal chain of thought lands on a reasoned answer rather than following a statistically likely pattern.

Deeper reasoning costs a longer, pricier think

Each extra pass through the network, each self-check, each search branch adds latency and GPU dollars. That is the trade: deeper reasoning for a longer, pricier think.

How a thinking machine is built

Four training ingredients, in order.

An LRM starts from a pre-trained LLM

An LRM usually builds on an existing LLM that has been through massive pre-training on billions of web pages, books, and code repos, which gives it language skills and a broad knowledge base.

Reasoning tuning teaches the model to show its work

After pre-training, the model is fine-tuned on curated data sets of logic puzzles, multi-step math problems, and tricky coding tasks, each with a full chain-of-thought answer key.

It learns to go from problem to plan to solution

Given a problem, its job is to come up with a plan, execute that plan in multiple steps, and arrive at a solution.

Fresh problems, with a reward for every step

Then the model is let loose on new problems under reinforcement learning. Either humans (RLHF) or process reward models judge each step of the chain as good or bad.

A teacher's reasoning traces train a smaller model

Distillation: a larger teacher model generates reasoning traces, and a smaller or newer model is trained on them.

The result can plan, verify, and explain

The shell-company prompt from the opening returns: this is the model built to make sense of it.

Schematic. RLHF is reinforcement learning from human feedback.

At runtime, how much thinking time do you give it?

Inference-time, or test-time, compute is spent every time you ask a question, and different questions get different amounts: "debug my stack trace" earns a good allowance, "write a fine caption" gets one quick pass.

A bigger budget buys several chains and a vote

During extended inference time a model may run multiple chains of thought and then vote on the best one.

It can backtrack, and call tools to spot-check

If a chain hits a dead end it may backtrack with a tree search, and it may call external tools, a calculator, a database, or a code sandbox, for spot checks.

Extra passes cost more; hopefully they buy accuracy

Each extra pass through the model costs more compute and a longer wait for the response. The hoped-for return is higher accuracy.

Worth it? It depends on the problem

Is the accuracy up-arrow worth the cost of getting it? It depends on the problem you are trying to solve.

LRMs excel at multi-step logic and planning

They do well on tasks that need multi-step logic, planning, or abstract reasoning, and they can internally verify and deliberate, so answers tend to be more nuanced and hopefully more accurate.

No need to say "let's think step by step"

LRMs usually need less prompt engineering. The magic words can stay out of the prompt because the model already does it: less prompt hackery.

The cost: more VRAM, more energy, slower replies

Higher computational cost means a higher invoice from your cloud provider, and the reply is slower while the model stops to think. In some situations a regular LLM or a smaller model is the better choice.

The highest-scoring models today tend to be LRMs

With LRMs, models are no longer spewing language as fast as they can predict the next word; they take time to think through a response. Today the models scoring highest on AI benchmarks tend to be the reasoning models.
