4  Text as Data: Naive Bayes

Week 4, 23 September

Until now, our datasets came with features already prepared. The UN data included income and urbanization, and Fearon and Laitin supplied measured and coded covariates. We began modeling after those measurement decisions had been made.

This week we start with 905 tweets. Before fitting a model, we need to turn their text into features. The choices involved are part of the analysis: they determine which information the classifier can use and can account for many of its errors.

We will use Naive Bayes. It assumes something about language that is clearly false, yet can still classify text effectively. In the second half of the session, we will examine how that is possible.

4.1 What Naive Bayes is good at, and what it is not

Benefits.

  • It fits by counting rather than optimization, so it is fast and can handle vocabularies of tens of thousands of words.
  • It can perform well with small training sets, useful when people must read and label every training document.
  • We can inspect the fitted word-frequency tables to see which words favor each label.
  • It returns probabilities that can be turned into classifications using a threshold suited to the task.

There are two important limitations.

First, the method assumes conditional independence between words. We will discuss why that assumption does not describe language well.

Second, the labels come from human coding decisions. Our classifier learns to reproduce one person’s application of a scheme for identifying immigration tweets. It does not independently define immigration, and any arbitrary parts of the coding scheme also shape its training.

4.3.2 The bag of words

We will use a simple representation. First, consider what it leaves out.

We represent each document by the words it contains, discarding order, grammar, and the relationships between words. “The wall stopped the caravan” and “the caravan stopped the wall” therefore have the same representation.

This is the bag of words approach: we retain the words and their counts, as though they had been mixed together in a bag.

Whether this is adequate depends on the research question. For detecting immigration content, words such as border, caravan, and asylum are often useful regardless of order. A question about who did what to whom would require a representation that preserves more of the relationships between words.

4.3.3 Cleaning, one decision at a time

The tm package stores documents in a corpus.

corpus <- VCorpus(VectorSource(tweets$text))

corpus[[1]]$content
[1] "....the FBI was in complete turmoil (see N.Y. Post) because of Comey’s poor leadership and the way he handled the Clinton mess (not to mention his usurpation of powers from the Justice Department). My firing of James Comey was a great day for America. He was a Crooked Cop......"

tm_map() applies a transformation to the corpus and returns the transformed corpus. We need to build one transformation ourselves to remove a pattern from the text.

replace_with_space <- content_transformer(function(x, pattern) {
  gsub(pattern, " ", x)
})

content_transformer() wraps our character-string function so that tm_map() can apply it to individual documents. Use this wrapper when creating your own cleaning function.

clean <- tm_map(corpus, content_transformer(tolower))
clean <- tm_map(clean, replace_with_space, "http[s]?://\\S*")
clean <- tm_map(clean, replace_with_space, "@\\S+")
clean <- tm_map(clean, replace_with_space, "[^a-z ]")
clean <- tm_map(clean, removeWords, stopwords("english"))
clean <- tm_map(clean, stemDocument)
clean <- tm_map(clean, stripWhitespace)

clean[[1]]$content
[1] "fbi complet turmoil see n y post comey s poor leadership way handl clinton mess mention usurp power justic depart fire jame comey great day america crook cop"

Here are the seven cleaning steps and the choices they involve.

Lowercasing combines Wall and wall, which we want, but also US and us, which we might want to keep separate. A general lowercasing rule cannot distinguish them.

Removing URLs and handles simplifies the text but can remove useful information. For example, @ThomasHomanICE, the acting director of Immigration and Customs Enforcement, is highly informative about a tweet’s subject. We omit it for a simple pipeline. In the workshop, you could test whether retaining handles improves classification.

[^a-z ] removes everything except lowercase letters and spaces: punctuation, digits, emoji, and the curly quotation marks and en dashes left behind by removePunctuation(). Without this step, some features appear in the output as codes such as <U+2019>, which can be confusing to interpret.

Stopwords are words such as the, and, of, and is, which usually carry little topical information. Inspect the supplied list with head(stopwords("english"), 50). It includes not and against, both of which can be important in political text.

Stemming combines inflected forms: immigration, immigrants, and immigrate become immigr. This pools sparse features, but the resulting stems are not ordinary words and some combinations may be unsuitable.

Whitespace cleanup is cosmetic. Unlike the other steps, it does not make a substantive choice about which information to retain.

The order of cleaning steps also matters. Removing punctuation first changes don't to dont, so it no longer matches the stopword don't. Reversing the steps changes the vocabulary. Denny and Spirling (Denny and Spirling 2018) show why preprocessing deserves more attention than a brief sentence in an appendix: these decisions can change substantive results.

4.3.4 The document-term matrix

dtm <- DocumentTermMatrix(clean)

dtm
<<DocumentTermMatrix (documents: 905, terms: 2735)>>
Non-/sparse entries: 16606/2458569
Sparsity           : 99%
Maximal term length: 16
Weighting          : term frequency (tf)

The document-term matrix has one row per document and one column per term. Entry \(i\), \(j\) counts how often term \(j\) occurs in document \(i\).

There are close to three thousand terms across 905 tweets, so we have more columns than rows. The matrix is also 99 percent sparse: most cells are zero, since a tweet uses only around fifteen of the available words.

These properties are common in text data. They create difficulties for a conventional regression with three thousand predictors and nine hundred observations. Naive Bayes handles them through the assumption introduced below.

This is a conditional probability: a rate within a subgroup. We are asking how common border is within each class, rather than across the corpus as a whole. Naive Bayes uses these class-specific proportions, which we can calculate from colSums().

The dashed line marks equal rates. A word on that line does not help distinguish the classes. Words farther from it provide more information, and the side of the line indicates which label they favor.

Most words are rare in both classes and cluster near the origin. Above the line are border, wall, southern, secur, and immigr, which favor immigration. Below it are words such as great, presid, and thank, which favor the other class. will is common in both and contributes little to distinguishing them.

We have not fitted the classifier yet, but this plot shows the rate comparison it will use.

4.4 Part II. Naive Bayes

4.4.1 The question the model asks

Given a tweet’s words, how likely is it to be about immigration? That is the question we want to answer.

Estimating every possible combination of words directly would require much more than our 724 training tweets. Most combinations have never appeared in those data.

Instead, we calculate how likely the words are under each label. The rates above provide those quantities, which we can compare to work back toward the label.

For a tweet containing border and wall:

  • Under the immigration label, these words are relatively common: about 50 and 27 occurrences per thousand words, respectively.
  • Under the other label, their rates are much lower: about 8 and 2 per thousand.

These words favor immigration. Bayes’ rule lets us use word probabilities conditional on a label to infer label probabilities conditional on the words. It also accounts for the relative frequency of the labels, so a rare label needs stronger evidence. We will use this intuition here; the further reading provides the algebra.

4.4.2 The naive assumption, which is false

We still need a way to calculate the probability of a whole combination of words. Naive Bayes simplifies this by assuming:

Assume that, within a class, each word appears independently of every other.

Under this assumption, knowing that an immigration tweet contains southern would tell us nothing further about whether it contains border.

That does not fit the language in this corpus. Southern and border often appear together, as do illegal and immigration, and caravan and migrant. Words are related to one another.

The assumption is useful computationally because it lets us multiply separate word rates instead of estimating the probability of every word combination. We can estimate those rates from a few hundred documents.

One consequence is overconfidence. A tweet with border, southern, wall, and patrol supplies correlated evidence, but the model treats the words as four independent signals. Its probabilities can therefore cluster near 0 and 1. Useful rankings do not imply calibrated probabilities. We may use these scores to order documents while being cautious about interpreting their confidence literally.

Before fitting, consider words that occur in one training class but never in the other.

Twenty-one words occur in immigration tweets but never in other tweets. Some are unsurprising, such as caravan, deport, daca, and migrant. Others, including stay and progress, may be absent from the other class simply by chance.

Their estimated rate in other would be zero. Since the model multiplies rates, one zero would make the whole product zero: caravan alone would rule out other, regardless of the remaining thirty words. Six thousand words of training text do not justify that certainty. Laplace smoothing adds one occurrence of every word to each class before calculating rates, preventing a single unobserved word from ruling a class out.

4.4.4 Fitting

Fitting takes two lines using the counts we have already calculated.

p_immigration <- (total_immigration + 1) / sum(total_immigration + 1)
p_other       <- (total_other + 1) / sum(total_other + 1)

Each vector gives the vocabulary’s word proportions within one class. The + 1 applies smoothing. Including it in the denominator keeps each vector’s entries summing to one.

We also need the prior probabilities: how common each label is. Without informative words, the model should favor the more common label.

prior_immigration <- mean(y_train == "immigration")
prior_other       <- mean(y_train == "other")

round(c(other = prior_other, immigration = prior_immigration), 3)
      other immigration 
      0.613       0.387

The model consists of two word-rate tables and two prior probabilities. There is no iterative optimization or convergence step, and no random seed needed for the fit. Unlike last week’s forest, we can inspect these fitted quantities directly.

round(1000 * c(immigration = p_immigration[["border"]],
               other       = p_other[["border"]]), 2)
immigration       other 
      43.77        7.10 
round(1000 * c(immigration = p_immigration[["great"]],
               other       = p_other[["great"]]), 2)

       9.75       17.74

border favors immigration, while great favors other. Smoothing gives caravan a small nonzero rate in other, so a tweet mentioning caravans can still receive that label if its remaining words support it strongly enough.

Note

The e1071 package provides naiveBayes(), but numeric matrix inputs are treated as normally distributed within each class. That is unsuitable for these word counts, most of which are zero; on this matrix it classifies almost every tweet as immigration. The calculations above implement the text-count version of Naive Bayes explicitly.

4.4.5 Predicting

For a new tweet, compare how well the two rate tables account for its words and assign the label with the higher score.

score_immigration <- as.vector(counts_test %*% log(p_immigration)) +
  log(prior_immigration)

score_other <- as.vector(counts_test %*% log(p_other)) +
  log(prior_other)

pred <- factor(ifelse(score_immigration > score_other, "immigration", "other"),
               levels = c("other", "immigration"))

head(pred)
[1] other       other       immigration other       immigration other      
Levels: other immigration

For each tweet, the matrix multiplication adds a word’s log rate under a label once per occurrence. Using border four times therefore contributes four times as much evidence as using it once. This is why we retained counts rather than just presence or absence.

Multiplying hundreds of small probabilities risks numerical underflow. Adding their logarithms avoids that problem while preserving the ordering of the scores, which is what we use for classification.

Applying the classifier to that stream would create more opportunities for false alarms and reduce performance.

caret reports several useful quantities. We introduce three here and return to them in Week 6.

No Information Rate is the majority-class baseline, accompanied by a test of whether the model beats it.

Sensitivity, or recall, is the proportion of immigration tweets the model identifies. Here it catches almost all of them.

Positive predictive value, or precision, is the proportion of flagged tweets that are genuinely about immigration. It is lower here: the model tends to over-flag rather than miss borderline cases.

Their relative importance depends on the task. If we want to estimate the immigration share of Trump’s output, false positives inflate it, making precision important. If we want to retrieve tweets for someone to read, recall may matter more, because false positives mainly add reading time.

4.4.7 The most informative words

For each word, compare its rates across the two classes. Using a log ratio puts evidence in both directions on a symmetric scale.

log_ratio <- sort(log(p_immigration / p_other))

top <- c(head(log_ratio, 12), tail(log_ratio, 12))

par(mar = c(4, 7, 3, 2))

barplot(top, horiz = TRUE, las = 1, cex.names = 0.8,
        col = ifelse(top > 0, "steelblue", "darkorange"),
        xlab = "log ratio, immigration versus other",
        main = "The words that move the classifier")

Unlike the forest’s importance ranking, this plot shows direction as well as strength. southern, apprehend, caravan, alien, humanitarian, and loophol favor immigration. impeach, witch, hunt, collus, and mueller favor the other class.

The orange words show a limitation. Impeachment and the Mueller investigation are not conceptually the opposite of immigration; they were other subjects Trump discussed in 2019. The classifier learns that distinction from this corpus. We should not assume it will transfer to another year or author.

These patterns suggest different responses: include word pairs to distinguish Wall Street, retain informative handles, and reconsider how to represent endorsement boilerplate. A bag of words alone cannot resolve that coding distinction.

4.4.9 The same model in caret

We wrote the calculations ourselves so you could see how the classifier works. For your own projects, a package is more convenient. We will use caret, as we did for the forest.

method = "naive_bayes" calls the naivebayes package, which needs to be installed.

install.packages("naivebayes")

We first change the features to two-level factors recording whether each word is present. As noted above, these functions otherwise treat numeric columns as normally distributed within classes, which poorly describes our mostly zero word counts.

as_yes_no <- function(counts) {
  out <- as.data.frame(counts > 0)
  out[] <- lapply(out, function(x) {
    factor(x, levels = c(FALSE, TRUE), labels = c("No", "Yes"))
  })
  out
}

X_train <- as_yes_no(counts_train)
X_test  <- as_yes_no(counts_test)

4.5 The politics of classification

The classification tasks in this course sort cases into categories. For political text, those categories concern speech.

Our classifier reads political speech and assigns a topic. Similar classification tasks appear in content moderation, media monitoring, and state surveillance. Thinking about those uses helps us see why even this small teaching example raises political questions.

Consider two aspects of the model we built.

First, someone chose immigration and other as the categories. The model cannot determine whether that was the right distinction for the research question. High accuracy does not justify the choice of categories.

Second, the errors have a pattern. False positives include tweets using immigration vocabulary for another purpose. In deployed systems, such patterns affect particular forms of speech and the people who use them. The consequences differ greatly between a measurement error in a paper and a classification that triggers a takedown, investigation, or visit.

We have asked who defines similarity and who selects the warning threshold. Here, ask who writes the codebook. Those decisions shape the model before fitting and may be difficult to see in its output.

4.6 Reading

Grimmer, J., and Stewart, B. M. 2013. “Text as Data: The Promise and Pitfalls of Automatic Content Analysis Methods for Political Texts.” Political Analysis 21(3): 267–297. (Grimmer and Stewart 2013)

This article introduces the field and several principles we have used: language models simplify language, no method is best for every task, and validation is essential. Read its discussion of supervised methods alongside Part II, paying attention to the problems it asks researchers to check.

Denny, M. J., and Spirling, A. 2018. “Text Preprocessing For Unsupervised Learning: Why It Matters, When It Misleads, and What To Do About It.” Political Analysis 26(2): 168–189. (Denny and Spirling 2018)

Read this alongside our cleaning steps. The authors show that preprocessing choices, including stemming, stopwords, and lowercasing, can change substantive conclusions, and that their order matters. Their analysis concerns unsupervised learning, but supervised pipelines also need scrutiny beyond an accuracy score.

Barberá, P., Boydstun, A. E., Linn, S., McMahon, R., and Nagler, J. 2021. “Automated Text Classification of News Articles: A Practical Guide to Using Supervised Machine Learning Methods.” Political Analysis 29(1): 19–42. (Barberá et al. 2021)

This is a practical companion to our exercise. Focus on the human coding stage, especially the number of coders and how to handle disagreement. Our corpus has only one coder, which limits what we can establish about the labels.

Recommended, not required.

Hopkins, D. J., and King, G. 2010. “A Method of Automated Nonparametric Content Analysis for Social Science.” American Journal of Political Science 54(1): 229–247. (Hopkins and King 2010)

Read this if your project will estimate category proportions rather than retrieve individual documents. The authors show why optimizing document-level accuracy can still produce biased proportions when errors do not cancel. That distinction matters for questions such as the share of tweets concerning immigration.

4.8 Questions for the session

Prepare an answer to at least two questions.

  1. The independence assumption is false of language and the classifier works anyway. What does that suggest about the relationship between the realism of a model’s assumptions and its usefulness? Is your answer the same for a predictive model as for an explanatory one?

  2. Our cleaning pipeline deleted @ThomasHomanICE, the handle of the acting director of ICE, whose presence is close to decisive evidence about a tweet’s topic. Defend the deletion. Then defend the opposite choice. Which argument would you actually make in a paper, and what would you have to report either way?

  3. The coding scheme decides that immigration mentioned as the seventh item in a list of accomplishments is other. A different coder could decide otherwise. Trace what would change: the labels, the model, the accuracy, and the substantive claim you would end up making about how much of Trump’s 2019 output concerned immigration.

  4. The classes were deliberately made roughly balanced, which makes accuracy more interpretable but means the corpus does not represent the full tweet stream. What would you change if your question concerned the share of Trump’s 2019 tweets about immigration rather than the classification of individual tweets?

  5. This handout built a machine that reads political speech and decides what it is about, using roughly forty lines of R and 724 labeled examples.
